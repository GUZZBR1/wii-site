# Perceived quality and continuity in interaction with camera-assisted drawing

## Abstract

A camera-assisted drawing system may produce a visually continuous response in some situations and still fail to match the experience of the person drawing. Technical measures and subjective ratings answer different questions; neither replaces the other. This article organizes four experience dimensions: continuity, predictability, recovery, and perceived control. For each dimension, it proposes an external measure and a questionnaire item to be refined with participants. The protocol combines a drawing task, observable records, an experience questionnaire, usability and workload measures, and an interview in the same session. Descriptive and associative analyses will examine whether technical patterns accompany reported perceptions, without assigning causality or inferring internal mechanisms. The contribution includes a measurement framework, a collection sequence, and limits on interpretation. Since no participant data are available, this article reports no scores, associations, or effects. The proposal requires ethics review, item evaluation, and data collection in defined contexts before it can support conclusions about user experience.

**Keywords:** quality of experience; continuity; usability; drawing; interaction

## 1. Introduction

A camera-assisted drawing activity brings together physical actions, materials, visual observation, and interpretation of the result. The person may perceive continuity when the drawing remains coherent during the activity. They may also notice delay, oscillation, temporary loss, slow recovery, or unexpected changes. These perceptions are part of the experience, but they cannot be deduced directly from a single technical measure. A record of regular response may coexist with an interaction that feels difficult; an observed interruption may be brief and not affect the person's goal.

Quality of experience, or QoE, describes the assessment a person makes of a service or interaction under conditions of use. The assessment combines context, expectations, task, and perception. External measures can characterize observable aspects of behavior. Questionnaires and interviews record the reported experience. The QoE and human-computer interaction literature proposes relating these sources, but emphasizes that an objective metric does not by itself represent satisfaction, trust, or perceived continuity. This challenge gains relevance in creative activities, in which the rhythm of action and the person's intention may change during the same session.

This article asks: **how do observable technical measures during a drawing task relate to the continuity, predictability, recovery, and perceived control reported by the participants?** The question is associative. It does not claim that a given measure causes a perception, nor that a subjective response reveals which technical condition produced it. Nor does it assume that participants share the same notion of continuity or control. The proposed items must be examined in accessible language and refined before any confirmatory collection.

The proposal delimits four related but distinct dimensions. Continuity describes whether the activity seems to proceed without unwanted breaks. Predictability concerns the possibility of anticipating the visible response. Recovery addresses the perception after an interruption or change. Perceived control describes whether the person considers that they can continue, pause, or adjust their action in an understandable way. Consistency and trust appear as auxiliary concepts that can contextualize the four dimensions, without extending the main model beyond the literature and the measures defined.

The contribution is methodological. First, it relates each dimension to an external measure and a questionnaire item, to make explicit the difference between observation and report. Second, it defines a protocol with a task, technical recording, and questionnaires in the same session, complemented by an interview. Third, it establishes an exploratory analysis by association and a simple model, with reporting of uncertainty and limits, without causal language. This article contains no data of its own; for this reason, the items are not presented as a validated scale, and no expected relation is reported as a result.

The proposal evaluates only visible events during the task and compares these records with the responses given by the person. The measures are formulated as observable properties, for example the duration of an interruption or the frequency with which the visible response stops following the activity. The final operational definition must be established before the study and applied in the same way to all comparable sessions.

The use of standardized questionnaires requires care. The System Usability Scale (SUS) provides an overview of perceived usability, while the NASA Task Load Index (NASA-TLX) characterizes reported workload. These instruments do not replace specific items on continuity and recovery. The items in this article are research proposals, not a ready-made scale. Their comprehension, relevance, and adequacy must be assessed with people who represent the intended contexts of use. Any adaptation of language or response mode must be documented so as not to presume equivalence with earlier applications.

Section 4 details the protocol, recruitment, collection, and analysis. Section 5 delimits what may be reported after collection. Section 6 interprets possibilities without anticipating results. ## 2. Related work

### 2.1 Quality of experience and perception

Quality of experience (QoE) concerns the subjective assessment of an interaction or service under defined conditions. Standardization work distinguishes the person's perception from the technical measures that characterize the session. The assessment depends on task, environment, expectation, and individual characteristics. This framework matters because an objective measure may help explain a perception, but does not determine it in a universal way. Research in telecommunications and interactive media proposes evaluation methods that combine description of the stimulus, presentation conditions, and subjective judgment [1–4].

In human-computer interaction, experience also includes dimensions that go beyond the instrumental success of the task. Usability, satisfaction, engagement, perceived ease, and trust may be related, but they are not interchangeable concepts [5, 6, 18–23]. A person may complete an action and still report discomfort. Another may accept an interruption when it is predictable and easy to recover from. The study separates task completion, observed behavior, and reported assessment so as not to condense these pieces of evidence into a single score.

A QoE metric must declare its unit of analysis and the moment of assessment. An evaluation after each task may capture an overall impression but depend on memory. A question immediately after an event may approximate the local perception, but interrupts the activity. A final interview offers context and unanticipated details, but may reconstruct episodes imprecisely. The proposed protocol combines measures aggregated per session and questions after short blocks, with a final interview to interpret divergences.

The relation between measure and perception may be linear, nonlinear, dependent on context, or absent in the sample [7–12]. Individual differences may reflect expectation, familiarity, strategy, purpose, or the condition of the session. For this reason, the protocol records the minimum relevant context and examines heterogeneity without attributing it to personal characteristics as a cause. The presentation of results must include dispersion and uncertainty, avoiding treating an average as the typical experience of all people.

### 2.2 Usability and workload

Usability describes whether specific people can reach goals with effectiveness, efficiency, and satisfaction in a defined context. The contextual definition prevents a property from being attributed abstractly to a system without making the task, audience, and environment explicit [5]. The SUS was proposed as a brief questionnaire for global assessment and has received studies on its use, interpretation, and measurement properties [24–26]. Its score does not identify, on its own, which aspect produced a high or low rating; for this reason, it must be accompanied by specific measures and comments.

The NASA-TLX was developed to assess perceived workload along distinct dimensions [27, 28]. The scale can help characterize the subjective demand of the task, but it measures neither continuity nor artistic quality. Studies of subjective load warn that different instruments may answer different questions and that the choice of method must follow the objective [29]. In this article, the NASA-TLX is an additional context: an experience perceived as discontinuous may be accompanied by greater reported effort, but this relation is a hypothesis to be tested, not evidence already obtained.

A responsible interpretation documents the version, language, mode of administration, timing, and adaptations of each instrument. The SUS should be presented according to the documented procedure, with any change indicated. The NASA-TLX may be administered in different ways; the option must be defined before collection. If a person prefers to answer orally or to use reading support, the protocol must record the adaptation and preserve voluntariness. Comparability with published data should not be presumed when the instrument or form of response has changed.

Global questionnaires do not capture each person's vocabulary or priorities. An interview may reveal that "continuity" means keeping the visible trace, not interrupting a conversation, or being able to resume after a pause. Specific items must be clear without imposing a rigid definition. It is recommended to check understanding through cognitive interviews or participatory review before treating them as confirmatory measures. The proposal also avoids turning individual responses into a diagnosis or a judgment of competence [30].

### 2.3 Continuity and flow in creative activities

Studies of temporal response in interfaces distinguish the interval between action and observable return, the regularity of the response, and the interruption of an activity. Research on digital ink interaction investigates how visual response takes part in the perception of writing and drawing [16–19]. These contexts are close because they involve movement and stroke, but their screen, pen, and task configurations differ from an activity on paper accompanied by a camera. The article uses this literature to formulate measurable dimensions, not to assume universal limits of acceptability.

Work on experience in interactive systems discusses engagement, context, interaction, and evaluation as related dimensions, without reducing experience to technical performance [18–23]. This article does not adopt "flow" as a variable, nor use it as a synonym for technical continuity or satisfaction. An activity may seem continuous without asserting a specific psychological state; it may also contain a desired pause without harming the experience. Interviews help distinguish unwanted interruption, rest, planned change, and external event.

Perceived continuity depends on expectation and task. A wait may be acceptable if the person understands that something is happening; a quick response may seem unpredictable if it changes without an apparent relation to the person's action. Recovery may matter more than the absolute absence of interruption, especially when the person can resume with an understanding of the state of the task. The literature on temporal experience, perceived response, and service quality models guides the vocabulary but does not provide a direct rule for a paper drawing task [2, 3, 16–19].

The gap that guides this article is the lack of a specific framework linking observable external measures during drawing to items asking about continuity, predictability, recovery, and control. Technical measures are necessary to describe what occurred, but they do not reveal the meaning of the occurrence. Questionnaires and interviews are necessary to capture interpretation, but they do not replace the description of the activity. The proposed protocol combines the sources in the same session and keeps the analysis associative.

## 3. Problem framing

The framework distinguishes observable measures from reported assessments. A technical measure describes a visible event and its occurrence under the defined task. A questionnaire item asks how the person interpreted or assessed the interaction. The pair does not claim that the two concepts are equivalent. It creates an analytical question that can be explored per session and per block of activity.

**Continuity** is the perception that the activity followed the drawing without interruptions that the person considered unwanted. An external measure may record periods in which the visible response stops following the observed action, as well as the duration and frequency of these occurrences. A pause planned by the person should not be classified as a failure of continuity. Context distinguishes intentional pause, rest interval, and unsolicited interruption.

**Predictability** describes whether the person can anticipate, based on the actions performed, when and how a visible response will occur. The proposed external measure characterizes the temporal variation between comparable actions and the corresponding visible responses. The item asks about perceived predictability, without suggesting that a stable distribution necessarily appears predictable. The person's expectation and familiarity with the task may change the interpretation.

**Recovery** describes the perception after an observable interruption or change. The external measure characterizes the interval between the end of a visible occurrence and the resumption of a response that allows the person to proceed. The protocol defines in advance which events count as start and end, without using internal information. The questionnaire item asks whether recovery was sufficient to resume the activity, rather than imposing a universal notion of speed.

**Perceived control** indicates whether the person feels able to choose when to continue, pause, repeat, or change their action. The external measure records voluntary pause and resumption actions, requests for help, and episodes in which the person tries to recover the activity. These records do not prove that the person had control; the perception must be asked about directly. A choice not to continue may be an autonomous decision, not a failure of the participant.

Consistency, interruption, confidence, and effort complement the four central dimensions. Consistency may be characterized by the variation of the visible response across comparable actions. Interruption describes events that break the task and were not planned by the person. Confidence to proceed is an assessment of intention, not a technical measure. Effort is subjective and may be contextualized through the NASA-TLX, without inferring capacity. These auxiliary dimensions help interpret the central pairs but must be analyzed separately.

## 4. Proposed evaluation protocol

The protocol combines external technical observation and subjective measures during the same session. The question, task, capture conditions, and annotation rules are defined before recruitment. The system is treated as a black box: the study does not record internal steps, private configurations, or non-observable states. The behavior of interest is the visible response that can be temporally associated with the person's action. A research camera or screen recording is used only when necessary, ethically approved, and authorized by the participant.

The study must obtain approval from the applicable ethics committee before recruitment contact. Invitation materials, consent, task, questionnaire, and interview must be available in accessible formats. The consent presents objectives, procedures, risks, data collected, access, retention, the possibility of recording, and the way to withdraw. Participation is voluntary. The person may refuse recording, pause, or end without penalty. The procedure describes an alternative for documenting the session when recording is refused, if that alternative preserves the methodological objective.

Recruitment seeks people with different drawing experience, familiarity with digital tools, and access needs relevant to the study. The team justifies inclusion and exclusion criteria, describes recruitment channels, and avoids defining disability as a mandatory diagnosis when the question does not require it. The composition of the sample is not invented in this proposal. The feasibility and possible diversity are assessed before collection and documented in the report. Sensitive characteristics are asked about only when necessary, with justification and the option not to answer.

Before the task, the team explains the procedure, offers an opportunity to try the materials, and asks which form of communication and response is preferred. The person chooses an activity compatible with the protocol, such as drawing a simple element or continuing a drawing of their own. The task does not depend on prior artistic skill. If the study requires a common instruction, it must be tested beforehand to ensure it does not favor a specific cultural repertoire. The person may adapt their strategy, with the context recorded.

Collection takes place in activity blocks defined so that measures and responses can be related without excessively interrupting the drawing. For each block, an external observer marks action, visible response, intervals, interruptions, voluntary pauses, resumptions, and requests for help. Times are extracted from a synchronized record and described in the unit and precision actually available, without reporting greater precision than the collection supports. Context notes indicate the task, material, chosen position, and external events that affected the session.

After each block, the person answers the items on continuity, predictability, recovery, and control that correspond to what has just happened. The wording uses the first person and concrete language. The team does not read a suggested interpretation as if it were an expected answer. If the block did not include a given occurrence, the participant may indicate that it does not apply. This response is not converted into either a positive or a negative assessment. The order of items and the timing of administration are defined before collection and kept consistent.

At the end, the SUS offers a general usability assessment and the NASA-TLX contextualizes perceived workload [24, 27, 28]. These instruments are kept separate from the specific items. The SUS is not used as a substitute for the dimensions of the framework; the NASA-TLX is not interpreted as a measure of performance. A semi-structured interview explores which events were noticed, what seemed predictable, what allowed the person to resume the task, and how the person defined control. Open questions help identify meanings of the proposed terms that differ from the intended ones.

Observers receive training with coding examples that do not include expected results. If two people code the records, agreement and disagreements will be assessed and discussed. The team keeps annotation rules, examples of ambiguous cases, and adjudication decisions. Consensus between coders is not presumed to eliminate subjectivity. Coding must capture observable events, while the person's interpretation remains a separate source.

The analysis begins with descriptions per participant, task, and block, including distribution, missing values, and context. Observable events and responses are aligned using the external time marking, with the synchronization uncertainty declared. Before examining associations, the team defines which dimension is primary and which comparisons are exploratory. This choice reduces the temptation to later select only the most favorable relation. The plan also records how "not applicable" responses and blocks in which the asked situation did not occur will be handled.

For association between ordinal scales or distributions that do not meet parametric assumptions, the team may opt for rank correlation. A Pearson correlation will only be considered when the variables and the form of the relation justify that use. The decision is documented before the final analysis. Estimates are accompanied by uncertainty intervals and substantive interpretation, not only by statistical significance. When observations from different blocks come from the same person, the dependence must be acknowledged. Data may be summarized per participant, or a model that represents clustering may be used if the design and the amount of data allow it.

The simple model relates one perceived dimension to an external measure chosen in advance. Context covariates should be few and justified by the question, such as self-reported familiarity with the task or the mode of participation. The model does not seek to explain the whole experience, nor to attribute causality. If the data are insufficient for stable estimates, the report is limited to descriptions and uncertainty intervals. The team does not invent a sample or an expected effect; feasibility and sample-size planning are defined before recruitment, based on the intended precision and the availability of participants.

Before the main collection, cognitive interviews or participatory review examine whether the questions are understood as intended and whether the dimensions make sense to different people. A pilot study verifies the session flow and the annotation rules. Resulting changes are documented; pilot data are only combined with the main collection when the decision is justified and compatible with the approved protocol. This process reduces ambiguities without presenting preliminary items as a validated measure.

## 6. Discussion

An association between an observable interval and the assessment of continuity would be compatible with the hypothesis that temporal aspects accompany the reported experience. Even so, correlation would not indicate that the interval caused the assessment. Expectations, intention, familiarity, material, environment, and chosen strategies could influence both measures. The analysis should report the pattern as a relation observed in the sample and indicate other plausible explanations.

A weak or absent association would also be informative, but it would not prove that the technical dimension is irrelevant. The external measure may not represent the event the person perceived; the item may not capture their vocabulary; the task may not have generated enough variation; or the context may have moderated the experience. Interviews and contextual records would help assess these possibilities. The article avoids concluding that people do not care about an aspect merely because a relation did not appear in the observed set.

If the questionnaires indicate continuity and the interviews describe interruptions, both sources must remain visible. The divergence may reflect an overall positive impression despite specific episodes, a different interpretation of "interruption," or a difference in memory between the moments of collection. The interview may also reveal that the person paused on purpose. The temporal unit and the definition of the questions guide the reading. A response is not corrected to make it coincide with the technical annotation.

A technically consistent response may be perceived as unpredictable when the person cannot anticipate the moment or meaning of the update. Conversely, external variation may be tolerated when the result is understandable and the task allows recovery. These scenarios justify examining predictability and recovery separately. The QoE literature distinguishes objective measures, the distribution of assessments, and contextual aspects [7–12]. The proposed study should test how these distinctions appear in this activity.

Perceived control is not equivalent to the number of voluntary actions. A person may choose few pauses because the task flows as they wish, or because they did not find a way to intervene. For this reason, observable action and subjective response are complementary. Asking how participants understand control, and what they would like to be able to do, prevents the researcher from imposing an interpretation based only on recorded events. This approach is compatible with experience studies that recognize context, expectations, and interaction as central dimensions [18–23].

The choice of temporal measures must remain tied to a task and to an annotation procedure. The literature on touch and digital ink interaction investigates perceived latency in specific configurations [13–17], but its values cannot be transferred directly to paper drawing accompanied by a camera. The proposal does not define a universal acceptability threshold. It proposes relating observations from the session to the same person's assessments and discussing how differences in task and context limit the comparison.

The SUS and the NASA-TLX offer useful general information, but they do not answer the central questions of this work [24–30]. A favorable general result may coexist with a low assessment of recovery. A report of high effort may stem from factors unrelated to continuity. The analysis must preserve the scopes and units of the instruments. If correlations between the general questionnaires and the specific items are presented, they will be exploratory and will not replace qualitative interpretation.

A possible practical contribution is to guide the review of tasks and quality criteria based on events that people actually consider relevant. This contribution requires including diverse people from the refinement of the items onward, not only at the end of the process. Items may need revision when participants interpret a word in an unexpected way. The team records this decision, examines whether earlier responses remain comparable, and delimits the scope of the conclusions.

Finally, the proposed association does not allow classifying systems, participants, or uses as good or bad without a justified standard. It offers a path for observing relations, identifying discrepancies, and formulating new studies. Reproducibility depends on event definitions, synchronization, tasks, items, and analysis being described clearly. Interpretation must recognize uncertain and heterogeneous results as part of the experience, rather than summarizing them in a single indicator.

## 7. Limitations and threats to validity

**Construct validity.** Continuity, predictability, recovery, and perceived control are proposed dimensions and do not constitute a validated scale. Preliminary items may not cover the experience that participants consider relevant. External measures may also represent only the visible events chosen by the protocol. Participatory review and cognitive interviews reduce ambiguities, but do not prove that all meanings are covered.

**Internal validity.** Measures and responses in the same session facilitate pairing, but observation and report may influence each other. Repeated questionnaires may interrupt the activity; recall of the block may be incomplete; the presence of a researcher or a recording may alter behavior. Familiarity with drawing, material, environment, and task may explain part of the relation. The observational design does not control all possible causes and does not permit a causal conclusion.

**External validity.** A convenience sample and a limited set of tasks do not represent all people, materials, or contexts. Free drawing, guided exercise, and collaborative task may produce different experiences. The availability of equipment and the environment also vary. The report must describe the context at the level necessary for interpretation, without promising population generalization or exposing identifiable data.

**Validity of instruments and data.** The SUS and NASA-TLX have defined purposes, but their language, format, and administration must be suited to the sample. Adaptations may reduce comparability. Proposed items may present response bias or ambiguity. Annotators may disagree about the start and end of an observable event. Predefined rules, training, coding audits, and documentation of missing data help make these uncertainties visible, but do not eliminate them.

**Statistical analysis.** Correlations may be influenced by observations dependent on the same person, limited distribution, or ordinal measures. The simple model should avoid an excess of predictors and be interpreted exploratorily when the sample does not support stable estimates. The team defines the strategy before examining relations, presents uncertainty, and avoids selecting only favorable associations. Null or imprecise results will not be described as proof of absence of a relation.

**Ethics and privacy.** Temporal records, audio, video, and interviews may contain identifiable information. The protocol collects only what is necessary, separates consents when appropriate, and restricts access. The person may refuse recording and still participate when the design allows. Ethics approval must precede any recruitment, and the final material must explain retention and disposal according to the applicable policy.

## 8. Conclusion

This article proposes relating observable technical measures to perceptions of continuity, predictability, recovery, and control during camera-assisted drawing activities. The matrix makes explicit the pairs between events and questionnaire items; the protocol combines external observation, general instruments, and an interview in the same session. The analysis is associative and preserves the difference between what was observed and what was reported.

There is no data to claim that the dimensions are related, nor that a given condition produces a particular experience. Before collection, the items must be reviewed with participants, the protocol must receive ethics approval, and the task must be tested in defined contexts. Future analysis should report uncertainty, individual differences, and divergent results without causality. Only then will it be possible to assess whether the framework explains relevant aspects of the experience in this activity.

Participatory refinement should verify whether the words of the items correspond to the situations that people observe and value. A pilot should confirm that the external record and the questions fit the flow of the task without imposing excessive pauses. The main study should separate measures by source, preserve divergent cases, and limit conclusions to the sample and the conditions observed. The proposal offers a structure for carrying out this evaluation, not a conclusion about the current quality of the experience.

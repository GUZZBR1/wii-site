# Reproducibility in evaluations of a camera-assisted drawing system

## Abstract

Computational system evaluations combine data, software, models, configurations, infrastructure, and analytical decisions. When these elements are not described sufficiently, readers may struggle to distinguish legitimate variation from undocumented change and to interpret the limits of a result. This article proposes a conceptual traceability protocol for evaluating reproducibility in studies of a camera-assisted drawing system. The proposal organizes records into six groups: data, models and external materials, configuration, environment, execution, and reporting. A checklist links each record to the scientific purpose it serves without prescribing internal system details. The protocol describes a comparison in which an experiment is repeated on different machines and dates, using criteria defined before data collection to classify agreement, divergence, and insufficient evidence. It also distinguishes repetition, replication, and reproduction while recognizing that communities use these terms differently. The article delivers an evaluation structure, descriptive measures, and validity threats to guide future studies. Since no measurements are presented, no degree of reproducibility is attributed to the system; empirical application and the choice of acceptable margins remain pending.

**Keywords:** reproducibility; traceability; research integrity; computational research; software evaluation

## 1. Introduction

Computational results depend on a chain of decisions that begins before the analysis and continues through execution and communication. The research question determines which observations matter. The selection of data, the inclusion criteria, the versions of the artifacts, the execution conditions, and the analytical choices delimit what the results can support. If these elements are scattered or implicit, an external team may find a conclusion without being able to assess how it was produced. This distance reduces the capacity to detect errors, compare studies, and decide whether a divergence reflects the phenomenon or a change in procedure.

The literature describes reproducibility problems in different fields, although the diagnoses are not interchangeable. Surveys of researchers have documented broad concern about the difficulty of reproducing findings, while methodological analyses have discussed how selection and reporting practices can weaken cumulative trust [6, 7, 15, 16]. In computational research, executing code can make calculations more transparent, but it also adds dependencies, environments, and software decisions that must be identified [8–11]. The mere availability of files, therefore, does not demonstrate that an evaluation can be interpreted or repeated. It is necessary to connect artifacts to specific questions, conditions, and claims.

This problem is particularly relevant for studies of visual interaction. An evaluation of a camera-assisted drawing system depends on scenes, materials, participants when present, devices, instructions, and annotation criteria. Small changes in these elements can alter the observation or its interpretation. At the same time, disclosing enough information to audit a study does not require exposing internal details of the system. The description can focus on the observable boundary between input conditions, recorded responses, and analysis, preserving the distinction between scientific documentation and implementation documentation.

The question of this article is: **which records and comparisons allow the reproducibility of studies of a camera-assisted drawing system to be assessed in an auditable way?** The aim is not to claim that any list guarantees identical results. The proposal seeks to make explicit what must be documented, how to structure a repetition attempt, and how to report both agreement and divergence without hiding uncertainty. The discussion considers that exact reproduction may be impractical when equipment, versions, contexts, or data cannot be kept constant.

The article offers three contributions. First, it organizes a checklist into six groups of records and relates each item to its usefulness for interpreting or auditing an evaluation. Second, it defines a conceptual protocol for repeating an experiment on different machines and dates, with analysis of variation and decision criteria documented in advance. Third, it presents a reporting structure that separates available evidence, inference, and information still missing. These contributions are methodological proposals, not results of an application already carried out.

Section 2 places the proposal within research on openness, data and model documentation, scientific computing, and the evaluation of machine learning systems. Section 3 defines traceability and presents the checklist. Section 4 specifies the conditions, records, measures, and proposed analysis. Section 5 describes the form of the results that a future application must fill in. Section 6 discusses possible interpretations; Section 7 delimits threats to validity; and Section 8 summarizes what must be done before any empirical conclusion.

The proposal also differentiates three products that are sometimes confused: documentation, operational repetition, and substantive confirmation. Documentation records how the study was conceived and carried out. Operational repetition examines whether another team can follow the declared procedure. Substantive confirmation assesses whether the evidence again supports the central interpretation. These products are related, but one does not automatically imply the other. A protocol may be well documented and still not be executable in a given context. A repetition may be executable and yet produce informative divergence. This distinction guides both the metrics and the language of the results, preventing the presence of shared materials from being presented as proof of empirical stability.

## 2. Related work

### 2.1 Reproducibility and transparency in empirical research

Reproducibility is not a simple property that can be inferred from a single file or from an openness statement. Opinion surveys and methodological debates have made visible the concern with results that are difficult to reproduce, but they have also warned that questions, definitions, and disciplines vary [6, 7]. The estimate conducted by the Open Science Collaboration examined repetition attempts in psychology and showed the importance of making explicit the design and the criteria used to compare results [7]. This work does not provide a rate transferable to evaluations of visual systems; its value here is methodological: success must be defined in relation to the question and to the available evidence.

Ioannidis discussed how biases, analytical flexibility, and low power can reduce the reliability of published findings [15]. In later work, he argued that incentives and institutional practices also affect the cumulative quality of evidence [16]. These texts support the need to distinguish transparency of the procedure from confirmation of the result. A complete report helps third parties examine decisions, but it does not eliminate bias, measurement error, or alternative explanations. The proposal of this article adopts this distinction and treats documentation as a condition for auditing, not as a guarantee of validity.

Open science principles recommend that methods, materials, and decisions be communicated in a manner compatible with public verification [13, 14]. The practical value of this commitment depends on context: data may contain sensitive information; materials may have licensing restrictions; and certain artifacts may stop working over time. A transparent statement must indicate what is available, what is restricted, and for what reason, without treating unavailability as evidence of misconduct. For studies with people, the protection of participants limits forms of sharing and requires alternatives, such as metadata, descriptions, and controlled-access procedures.

Computational research adds a further question: results derived from code are arguments whose chain of transformation must be examinable. Peng proposes associating computational articles with compendia that allow data, code, and results to be inspected [8]. Sandve and colleagues present practical recommendations for making computational analyses more reproducible [9]. McNutt emphasizes the editorial and institutional responsibility to raise the transparency of reporting [14]. Taken together, these works support a view of reproducibility as a property of the research process and its documentation, and not as a synonym for publishing a repository.

### 2.2 Computational research and software artifacts

Good practices in scientific computing treat organization, testing, version control, review, and documentation as components of empirical work [10, 11]. Such practices make it more likely that decisions are explicit and that errors are identifiable. They do not replace the description of the experimental design: a well-organized program may execute an inadequate analysis, and a computable result may depend on poorly documented data or assumptions. For this reason, the list proposed in this article includes both properties of the artifacts and relations between question, protocol, and claim.

The citation of software is another necessary component. Smith, Katz, and Niemeyer argue that software used in research should receive appropriate credit and identification [12]. A reference to a project without a version or persistent identifier may be insufficient to know which artifact was used. Research on software studies also investigates the possibility of reconstructing published analyses from the materials made available [23]. Mappings of replications and follow-up studies indicate that availability, executability, and correspondence between materials and reporting are distinct problems [24, 25]. This literature helps avoid a narrow definition according to which providing code is enough to claim that a study is reproducible.

Stodden and Miguez highlight that infrastructure and software environments influence the extent and reliability of computational research [26]. Baiocchi discusses guidelines for computational research in economics, emphasizing the integration of text, code, data, and open-source software when possible [27]. Packaging tools can support the description of environments, as illustrated by Boettiger's introduction to the use of containers [20]. The specific tool, however, does not by itself resolve data management, hardware limitations, the future availability of services, or the documentation of analytical decisions. An isolated environment may reduce installation differences without making an external measurement equivalent on other equipment.

Practices for computational notebooks have also been systematized into rules for writing and sharing analyses [21]. Fomel and Claerbout place electronic documents and reproducible computations as a change in how scientific evidence is communicated [22]. Works on artifacts in software engineering complement these recommendations with the problem of re-executing and interpreting published materials [23–25]. The relevant gap is the lack of a concise protocol that connects these principles to an experimental evaluation of visual interaction, without turning the article into an installation manual or an internal description of the product.

### 2.3 Evaluation of machine learning and computer vision systems

FAIR principles propose that data be findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable, with attention to the conditions that make each objective feasible [1]. Later discussions question how to operationalize these principles and what may be considered sufficient in different contexts [2, 3]. For this article, FAIR offers guidance for data and metadata, but it does not alone cover experimental execution, the validity of instruments, or the interpretation of metrics. The checklist incorporates traceability and access, without presuming that every material can or should be opened without restriction.

Datasheets for datasets organize information about motivation, composition, collection, processing, uses, and limitations [4]. Model cards propose documenting the purposes, evaluation conditions, performance, and limitations of models [5]. Both help make assumptions and contexts visible. Application to a system study requires caution: a study may use external data and models, but its unit of evaluation also includes the protocol, the environment, and the analysis criteria. Data or model documents do not replace the complete report of the experiment, nor do they assert that the evaluated resource works for uses that were not tested.

In artificial intelligence, Gundersen and Kjensmo mapped reproducibility problems and proposed greater attention to the description of experiments [19]. Heil and colleagues present reproducibility recommendations for machine learning in the life sciences [17]. Haibe-Kains and colleagues discuss transparency and reproducibility in artificial intelligence, observing that scientific trust requires access and reporting sufficient to examine analyses [18]. Beam, Manrai, and Ghassemi describe reproduction challenges for machine learning models applied to health, where data, configurations, and populations may limit comparison [29]. Although the clinical domain is different, the warning applies in general: changes in the dataset or in the evaluation population can alter the interpretation of a comparison.

Pineau and colleagues report on the NeurIPS reproducibility initiative and the adoption of a checklist for machine learning papers [28]. Desai and colleagues discuss the meaning of reproducibility in artificial intelligence and machine learning research [30]. These works show that checklists are useful when they are connected to concrete publication and evaluation decisions. Even so, binary questions may hide differences between missing information, not applicable information, and legitimate restriction. The checklist of this article allows these situations to be indicated and requires justification when an item cannot be provided.

Thus, the gap is not the absence of principles, checklists, or studies on reproducibility. It is the need for a framework applied to evaluations of a camera-assisted drawing system that combines the recording of materials, comparison between executions, and interpretation of results. The proposal translates general guidance into auditable items, establishes categories for reporting variation across conditions, and makes explicit that no checklist replaces data, appropriate instruments, or scientific judgment.

## 3. Problem framing

In this article, reproducibility means the possibility for a team to assess whether a result can be obtained again under described conditions and whether any differences can be interpreted. The definition includes more than numerical repetition. A result may vary between executions and still be informative, provided the variation is measured, justified, and related to the question. There may also be apparent agreement without substantive reproducibility, for example when two teams compute the same metric on different data without recognizing the change. The unit of analysis, therefore, is the relation between a claim, the evidence that supports it, the registered conditions, and the applied analysis.

Traceability is the capacity to follow this relation throughout the study. Each conclusion must point to an analysis; each analysis, to observations and definitions; and each observation, to identifiable conditions and materials. The chain can be summarized in four elements: question, protocol, records, and report. Figure 1 presents this link as a conceptual structure. It does not represent internal stages of a system. Its purpose is to show that scattered documentation prevents the interpretation of how a claim connects to the procedure that produced it.

A checklist is useful when it serves an observable decision, rather than functioning as a bureaucratic inventory. Each item must answer two questions: what must be recorded to interpret the evaluation, and why is this record necessary? Table 1 groups items into six areas. The set does not presume that all items apply to every study. The report must mark items as applicable, not applicable, or restricted, explaining the justification. Information that is not available must not be silently converted into “not applicable”.

The six groups cover related, but not identical, elements. Data refers to the observations analyzed and to the processes used to produce them. Models and external materials include resources that may be obtained from other teams or distributors. Configuration describes controllable choices for the evaluation, without enumerating internal details of the system. Environment records characteristics of the computational and physical context. Execution documents what occurred in each session. Reporting gathers the definitions, analyses, and limitations that allow the evidence to be interpreted. This separation helps locate gaps without suggesting that any single category explains every divergence.

An additional conceptual difference involves the terms repetition, replication, and reproduction. Some communities reserve “repeat” for the same team and the same materials, “replicate” for another team with the same protocol, and “reproduce” for an independent implementation. Others use the terms differently or swap their definitions. The article does not impose a universal taxonomy. It proposes that authors define the terms before presenting a comparison. Figure 2 illustrates one possible convention and makes visible that the categories vary by team, materials, and implementation.

Acceptable variation cannot be chosen after observing the results without justification. It depends on the purpose of the metric, the precision of the instrument, the expected variability, and the cost of an incorrect decision. For continuous metrics, an equivalence margin requires substantive interpretation and justification made before the comparison. For categorical metrics, the protocol must declare how disagreements and missing data will be treated. If there is no basis for fixing a margin, the study must present the observed distribution and classify it as descriptive, without claiming equivalence.

Traceability also does not mean storing all data indiscriminately. Participant data, incidental images, and information that could reveal identity must be handled according to consent, ethical approval, and applicable restrictions. Metadata, aggregate statistics, and procedure descriptions can support auditing without exposing sensitive material. The decision to restrict access must be recorded with its justification and, where possible, accompanied by a controlled route for consultation. Thus, transparency and protection are not automatically incompatible goals, but they require explicit decisions.

## 4. Proposed evaluation protocol

The protocol proposes a conceptual case study: a team repeats the evaluation of a camera-assisted drawing system on different machines and dates. The comparison seeks to determine whether the procedure can be followed, whether the observations are interpretable, and how the measures vary. It is not assumed that two executions will produce identical values. The team defines in advance what it considers a repetition, which evidence will be recorded, which metrics answer the question, and which results may be classified as inconclusive.

**Conditions and design.** The evaluation describes a delimited visual task, the materials presented, the instructions, and the observable initial state. The scene sample must reflect the question and not merely convenient cases. When participants are present, the protocol reports recruitment, consent, inclusion criteria, and relevant characteristics in a manner compatible with identity protection. When observations are prepared scenes without participants, the team explains how they were built and which variations they intend to represent. The article does not anticipate results or fix performance levels.

The first execution establishes the reference procedure. A team records the protocol, the materials, and the analysis decisions before starting the repetition. Another execution takes place on a different date and on equipment of a different class, maintaining the conditions that the study defined as necessary for comparison. The equipment class, the physical context, the identifiable version of the materials, and any deviation must be recorded. An unavoidable change does not automatically invalidate the comparison; it limits interpretation and must be treated as a potential factor of variation.

**Data and record integrity.** Each set of observations receives a persistent identifier within the scope of the study. The data notebook describes origin, collection, annotation, exclusions, and restrictions. Annotations are accompanied by operational definitions and by a procedure for resolving disagreements. If data cannot be shared, the report explains the restriction and indicates which summaries, metadata, or controlled reviews can be made available. Models and external materials are identified by origin, version or date, and purpose. None of this information should expose internal mechanisms of the evaluated system.

**Execution and conditions.** For each session, the date, responsible team, equipment class, evaluation configuration, observable duration, interruptions, and deviations are recorded. The order of conditions is defined before collection or randomized when this is compatible with the design. If there is a risk of learning, fatigue, or scene change, the protocol explains how these effects will be handled. An interrupted session is not discarded without a recorded reason. The team distinguishes operational failure, absence of observation, and negative result, since these events have different meanings.

**Metrics.** The protocol separates measures of documentary completeness, agreement between repetitions, and auditability. Completeness is described per applicable item: filled, missing, or restricted, always with justification. Agreement is summarized by paired differences between executions for continuous measures and by agreement tables for categories. When suitable for the design, uncertainty intervals are estimated by a declared procedure. A single composite score is not used to replace the interpretation of the items: two executions may agree on one metric and diverge on another. **Analysis.** The analysis begins by verifying whether the executions match the declared protocol. The team documents divergent items and decides whether each difference is compatible with the planned comparison. It then presents descriptive statistics by condition, differences between executions, and uncertainty, without reducing the results to a binary statement of “reproduced” or “not reproduced”. If a justified equivalence margin exists, the comparison may assess whether the differences remain within it. Otherwise, the observed dispersion is reported and declaring equivalence is avoided. Divergences are investigated on the basis of the available records, but an association between a change and a difference does not establish causality.

**Threats and analytical safeguards.** Variation between machines may reflect differences in equipment, environmental conditions, or collection procedures, and not a single cause. Changes between dates may coincide with alterations in the materials or in the people involved. The protocol records these factors and avoids attributing a difference to any one of them without adequate comparison. The team also pre-specifies exclusion criteria, handling of absences, primary outcomes, and analysis. Necessary changes are recorded with date and justification. This discipline does not prevent adaptive decisions, but it allows readers to distinguish the initial plan from later choices.

Finally, an independent review verifies whether the described materials correspond to the report and whether an external person can locate the evidence associated with each claim. This review does not need to reconstruct the system. Its object is the study: clarity of the question, access or restriction of the materials, coherence between protocol and analysis, and presentation of uncertainties. The proposal must be registered before data collection, including those responsible, consents, and access conditions. Empirical application remains necessary to assess whether these items are practicable and sufficient in real studies.

## 5. Structure of the results

An application of the protocol should begin by reporting which checklist items were applicable, filled in, missing, or restricted, and which justifications were provided. This synthesis will not be a quality scoreboard. Its function is to show that evidence is available to interpret the comparison. The report should describe the executions carried out, the conditions that remained constant, the changes between sessions, and the deviations from the protocol. Every relevant difference must be presented together with the record that allows it to be examined.

The result measures will include differences between executions for each previously selected outcome, dispersion summaries, and uncertainty intervals when compatible with the design. For categorical data, the categories compared and the observed disagreements will be reported. For missing data, the report will indicate their known origin or state that the cause could not be determined. Equivalence will not be inferred merely from the absence of a statistically detectable difference. If no justifiable equivalence margin exists, the results will be treated as descriptive.

Figure 3 offers only a visual orientation. The expected shape, not a result, and it should not be interpreted as an observed trend. A future empirical version should present only observations actually collected, with a legend identifying conditions, measure, uncertainty, and exclusions. ## 6. Discussion

The proposed structure treats reproducibility as an empirical question and not as an attribute conferred by the authors' intention. A first possible outcome would be agreement between executions under the planned conditions. This result would suggest that, for that protocol and context, the observed variation is compatible with the previously justified margin. It would not demonstrate that the system works on every device, task, or population. The inference would depend on the coverage of conditions, the precision of the measures, and the quality of the records. A restricted agreement must be reported as such.

A second possible outcome would be agreement on some measures and divergence on others. This would indicate that the evaluation is sensitive to certain outcomes or conditions, while others remain stable within the observed scope. A useful interpretation would examine whether the measures differ in scale, sensitivity, or contextual dependence. The conclusion must not compress mixed patterns into a global label of success or failure. This recommendation is aligned with the literature that separates availability of artifacts, transparency of decisions, and validity of a conclusion [17–19, 28].

A third outcome would be a broad divergence between executions. It may reveal insufficient documentation, context changes, legitimate variation, or measurement failures. The protocol allows these possibilities to be distinguished only when the records support the investigation. If data are missing about a change, attributing the divergence to a specific cause would exceed the evidence. A divergence replicated under documented conditions may, on the other hand, show that the behavior varies and that the isolated average does not adequately describe the system. The empirical software literature reinforces the importance of making interpretable packages available and of examining what is effectively reconstructible [23–26].

A fourth outcome would be insufficient evidence. This may occur if materials are not accessible, if metric definitions are ambiguous, if changes between sessions are not recorded, or if the number and coverage of observations do not answer the question. “Insufficient” is not equivalent to “not reproduced”. The distinction prevents documentary gaps from being converted into a claim about the behavior of the system. It also guides the next research step: revising the protocol, completing the records, or expanding collection, depending on the source of the insufficiency.

The proposal engages with FAIR, datasheets, and model cards by turning documentation principles into questions about provenance, context, and limits [1–5]. These instruments are complementary. A datasheet describes a dataset; a model card contextualizes a model; FAIR organizes objectives for management and reuse. None of them, in isolation, accompanies all the decisions of an interaction evaluation. The checklist incorporates these practices without treating them as quality certification. The comparison with machine learning checklists also shows that a list has greater value when each item asks for evidence or justification, rather than an abstract confirmation [17, 19, 28, 30].

The application to a camera-assisted drawing system offers a bounded case for testing this structure. Its scientific value will depend on assessing whether the items are understandable, whether external teams can fill them in, and whether the measures distinguish documentary changes from variation in results. This requires a real study, review by people who did not write the protocol, and adjustments before proposing generalizations. The article does not anticipate this result. Its argument is that traceability can make the conditions of interpretation more explicit and that this claim must be evaluated, not presumed.
 A careful reading must also distinguish a divergence that changes the practical decision from another that changes only the presentation of a summary. This distinction will depend on the intended use and must be justified before the analysis. By publishing both compatible and divergent patterns, the study may contribute to refining future protocols without converting a single case into a universal recommendation.

## 7. Limitations and threats to validity

Internal validity may be threatened by simultaneous changes in equipment, environment, materials, and team. If the executions differ in several respects, the comparison cannot identify which difference explains the variation. A record checklist helps to observe changes, but does not control them. The protocol can reduce this ambiguity through planning and documentation, but the decision to keep or vary each condition depends on the question. The order of tasks, learning, fatigue, and interruptions may also influence observations when people participate. The application must make these possibilities explicit and not attribute causal effect without an adequate design.

Construct validity depends on the adequacy of the measures to the concept of reproducibility. Documentary completeness measures the presence of records, not their truth or sufficiency. Numerical agreement measures similarity according to a metric, but it may hide important differences in experience or in the distribution of errors. An equivalence margin without justification may give the appearance of rigor to an arbitrary decision. The analysis must relate each measure to the question, report unit and uncertainty, and preserve disaggregated results when a synthesis could hide distinct patterns.

External validity is limited by the choice of tasks, conditions, and equipment. A repetition on different machines constitutes evidence for those classes and conditions, not for every environment of use. Studies with convenience samples, prepared scenes, or few contexts may not represent everyday uses. Transfer to other tasks, populations, or contexts requires additional evidence. The documentation principles cited are general, but the usefulness of each item may vary by domain, data availability, and institutional restrictions.

Data validity involves annotation quality, coverage of conditions, losses, privacy, and version changes. Annotations may diverge even with shared instructions. Unavailable data reduce the possibility of auditing, but their restriction may be necessary to protect participants or comply with licenses. A public description of the criteria and restrictions improves transparency, although it does not allow inaccessible observations to be reproduced. The study must separate what was preserved, what can be shared, and what remains impossible to verify.

Finally, the proposal has not yet been applied or evaluated by independent teams. The taxonomy may omit items or group together, in an unhelpful way, decisions that researchers would make separately. The protocol may impose a documentation cost greater than its benefit in small exploratory studies. The list may need adaptation after pilots, provided that changes are justified and not presented as universal requirements. The reviewed literature also does not exhaust the available practices. These limitations make the proposal a basis for methodological evaluation, not a validated standard.
 A pilot may test the clarity of the items, the filling-in burden, and the ability to locate records, but this pilot will still not demonstrate external validity. The final version should indicate which changes resulted from this test and which questions remain open.

## 8. Conclusion

This article proposed a framework for assessing the reproducibility of studies of a camera-assisted drawing system. The framework organizes records of data, external materials, configuration, environment, execution, and reporting; defines a comparison between executions on different machines and dates; and separates agreement, divergence, and insufficient evidence. It also distinguishes documentation, operational repetition, and substantive confirmation, avoiding treating the availability of materials as a guarantee of validity.

The protocol still requires empirical application, independent review, and contextual justification for margins of variation. Without these steps and without collected data, it is not possible to claim that the proposed evaluation works or that the system is reproducible. A future study must fill in the pending fields, document deviations, and restrict conclusions to the conditions actually observed.
 It must also choose a definition of repetition suited to its community and state how the team will handle changes in materials or access restrictions. If the records allow only a partial comparison, this limitation must appear alongside the conclusion. In this way, the protocol may be assessed by the extent to which it clarifies decisions and exposes gaps, without promising identity between executions.
